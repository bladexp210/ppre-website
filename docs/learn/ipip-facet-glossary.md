# IPIP facet glossary

Plain-English lines for hover tooltips. Facet names match `data/ipip120-scoring-key.md`. A high score means more of what the sentence describes. These are everyday tendencies, not medical diagnoses.

## Traits

**Neuroticism.** How readily a person feels worry, hurt, or stress, and how long those feelings stay.

Examples: A short text with no emoji can nag at them for an hour. A hard comment is still in their head the next morning. A crowded deadline makes their stomach tight before the day starts.

**Extraversion.** How much a person seeks people, activity, and a lively pace.

Examples: They would rather meet a friend than spend the evening alone. A quiet Sunday feels too still, so they call someone. They perk up when a room gets noisy.

**Openness.** How much a person seeks new ideas, feelings, art, and change.

Examples: They take a new street home just to see it. A strange idea is interesting before they ask if it is useful. They notice the feeling in a song, not only the tune.

**Agreeableness.** How warm, fair, and easy to get along with a person tends to be.

Examples: They assume a late friend was held up, not rude. They give someone the benefit of the doubt in a disagreement. They would rather smooth it over than win the point.

**Conscientiousness.** How much a person follows through, keeps order, and thinks before acting.

Examples: They finish the form today instead of leaving it overnight. The keys go back in the same place. They read the steps once before they begin.

## Neuroticism

**Anxiety.** How readily a person worries and feels tension before anything specific has gone wrong.

Examples: They rehearse a meeting that has not happened yet. A quiet phone can feel like bad news. They tense up while waiting for a result that is probably fine.

**Anger.** How fast frustration turns into irritation, and how easily a person loses their temper.

Examples: A slow queue makes their jaw tight within a minute. A small mistake at home comes out as a sharp reply. They snap, then wish they had waited.

**Depression.** How often a person feels low, discouraged, or unhappy with themselves. This is an everyday mood tendency, not a medical diagnosis.

Examples: A gray afternoon feels heavier than it looks. After a small mistake they are hard on themselves for the rest of the day. A good moment fades sooner than they expected.

**Self-Consciousness.** How exposed a person feels around others, and how much they would rather stay unnoticed.

Examples: They rehearse a sentence before they say it in a group. Walking into a party, they hope nobody looks over. They would rather listen than be the one who speaks.

**Immoderation.** How hard it is to stop once a craving or an urge gets going.

Examples: One biscuit becomes the rest of the packet. They meant to check one message and an hour is gone. A sale turns into buying more than they planned.

**Vulnerability.** How easily pressure floods a person, and how hard it is to stay steady when things pile up.

Examples: Two deadlines on the same day leave them unable to start either. A harsh email makes it hard to think about the next task. When plans change suddenly, they need a minute before they can respond.

## Extraversion

**Friendliness.** How quickly a person warms to others and lets them get close.

Examples: They chat with the person next to them in a queue. A new neighbor gets a hello on the first day. They remember a colleague's small story and ask about it later.

**Gregariousness.** How much a person wants a crowd, a party, or a room full of people.

Examples: They choose the big table over dinner for two. A full weekend with no plans to see anyone feels flat. They stay late at a party because the room is still busy.

**Assertiveness.** How readily a person speaks up, takes the lead, and tells others what to do.

Examples: In a stuck meeting they say what they think the group should do. They ask for the seat, the raise, or the turn. When nobody picks a restaurant, they pick one.

**Activity Level.** How full and fast a person likes their days to be.

Examples: An empty afternoon makes them look for something to do. They stack errands so the day stays moving. A slow weekend is pleasant only if they still have a project on.

**Excitement-Seeking.** How much a person wants thrills, adventure, and a bit of risk.

Examples: They pick the faster, louder option when both are on offer. A familiar holiday feels dull unless there is something new to try. They say yes to a ride, a hike, or a last-minute trip.

**Cheerfulness.** How often a person feels joyful and looks on the bright side.

Examples: They laugh easily at a small joke. A setback is annoying, then they look for the workable part. People notice that they walk in already in a good mood.

## Openness

**Imagination.** How readily a person daydreams and enjoys ideas that are far from everyday life.

Examples: They invent a whole story while waiting for the bus. A blank page turns into a scene that could not happen in real life. They enjoy a film most when it leaves ordinary life behind.

**Artistic Interests.** How much a person is drawn to art, beauty, music, and poetry.

Examples: They stop to look at a painting other people walk past. A song matters for how it sounds, not only for the words. They will cross town for a gallery, a concert, or a poem they have not read.

**Emotionality.** How strongly a person notices their own feelings and the feelings of people around them.

Examples: A sad scene in a film stays with them after it ends. They can tell a friend is upset before the friend says so. Their own mood is easy for them to name.

**Adventurousness.** How much a person prefers new places and new routines over what is already familiar.

Examples: They order the dish they have not tried. A new city is more appealing than the same hotel again. They change a weekly habit just to see how it feels.

**Intellect.** How much a person enjoys hard ideas, abstract questions, and challenging reading.

Examples: They pick up a difficult book on purpose. A "why is that true?" question is fun, not a chore. They will follow an argument that takes several steps.

**Liberalism.** How much a person prefers changing social rules over keeping tradition as it is.

Examples: They question a rule whose only defense is "we have always done it this way." They are uneasy with one fixed answer for everyone. They would rather loosen an old penalty than keep it unchanged.

## Agreeableness

**Trust.** How readily a person assumes other people mean well and are telling the truth.

Examples: They believe the explanation a stranger gives them. A missed call is more likely a busy friend than a snub. They lend a small thing without assuming it will not come back.

**Morality.** How unwilling a person is to cheat, use others, or bend the rules for their own gain.

Examples: They will not copy an answer even when nobody would notice. They tell the cashier about an extra item in the bag. They refuse a shortcut that only works if someone else loses.

**Altruism.** How gladly a person spends time and effort helping other people.

Examples: They stay late to help a colleague finish. They carry a neighbor's bags without being asked. They give up a free hour because someone else needs it.

**Cooperation.** How much a person avoids fights, insults, and getting even.

Examples: They let a rude comment pass instead of firing one back. In an argument they look for a way both people can live with. They do not keep score after a slight.

**Modesty.** How little a person boasts or treats themselves as better than other people.

Examples: They mention a success and then change the subject. They share credit with the people who helped. Praise makes them point to the work, not to themselves.

**Sympathy.** How easily a person feels for people who are having a hard time.

Examples: A news story about a stranger stays with them. They notice when a coworker is having a bad week and ask. They find it hard to walk past someone who is clearly struggling.

## Conscientiousness

**Self-Efficacy.** How confident a person is that they can take on a task and finish it well.

Examples: A new tool looks learnable, not frightening. They say "I can sort that" and then do. A hard first draft does not convince them they should stop.

**Orderliness.** How much a person likes things tidy and put back where they belong.

Examples: The desk is cleared before they leave. Cables, tools, and papers have a place. A messy room makes it harder for them to start.

**Dutifulness.** How seriously a person keeps promises and tells the truth.

Examples: They show up at the time they named. They say the awkward true thing instead of an easy false one. A promise made on Tuesday is still in force on Friday.

**Achievement-Striving.** How hard a person pushes to do more than the minimum.

Examples: They revise a piece that was already good enough to send. A target is a starting line, not a place to stop. They stay with a goal after the required part is done.

**Self-Discipline.** How well a person starts a task and stays with it when it gets boring.

Examples: They open the dull task instead of circling it. They come back to it after a distraction. They finish the repetitive part without waiting to feel like it.

**Cautiousness.** How often a person thinks a step through before jumping in.

Examples: They read the whole message before they reply. They check the time, the cost, and the exit before they say yes. They pause when everyone else is already moving.

## Statistical terms

**Mean.** The average score for a group. Add the scores and divide by how many people are in that group.

Examples: Scores of 2, 3, and 4 have a mean of 3. If ten people average 3.2 on anxiety, 3.2 is the mean. One very high score can pull the mean up even when most people sit lower.

**Standard deviation.** How spread out the scores are around the average. A small number means most people sit close together. A large number means some score much lower and some much higher. If the typical spread is 10 points, most people fall within about 10 points of the average.

Examples: If the average height is 170 cm and the spread is 7 cm, most people fall between about 163 and 177 cm. A spread of 0.4 on a 1-to-5 scale means the group clusters tightly. A spread of 1.2 on that same scale means people are scattered from low to high.

**Overlap percentage.** How much of the two groups' scores fall in the same range. A high percentage means the groups mostly look alike.

Examples: An overlap of about 90 percent means the two groups mostly cover the same scores. An overlap near 50 percent means a large part of each group sits apart from the other. Two curves that lie on top of each other are close to 100 percent overlap.

**Cohen's d.** How far apart the two averages are, counted in steps of the typical spread. See docs/index.md. A d of 0.50 means the average man is half a typical spread away from the average woman, like the average man being taller than about 70 percent of women.

Examples: A d of 0.20 is like a height gap so small you would not pick it out across a room. A d of 0.50 is that half-step gap: the average man taller than about 70 percent of women. A d of 0.80 is a large difference: the higher average sits above about 80 percent of the other group.

**Sample size.** How many people's answers the number is based on. A small sample is a thinner basis for the comparison. See docs/index.md.

Examples: A mean from 40 people is a thinner basis than a mean from 4,000. If 80 people fall in an age band, the sample size for that band is 80. A larger sample is less likely to swing because a few unusual people happened to be included.
